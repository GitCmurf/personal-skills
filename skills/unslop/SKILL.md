# Unslop

Reduce **reader cost**: effort the reader spends without gaining meaning. Make
the prose clear and pleasant to read without replacing the author's thought
with the agent's personality.

## Contract

- Work fidelity-first. Preserve meaning, facts, factual force, uncertainty,
  attribution, the author's position, and any requested structure.
- Honour the requested operation. Review when asked to review; rewrite only
  when asked to draft, edit, or rewrite.
- Do not invent opinions, evidence, confidence, sources, or emotional colour.
- Preserve code, commands, structured data, citations, links, filenames,
  identifiers, formal terms, and verbatim text unless the user explicitly asks
  to change them.
- Use British English by default. An explicit audience, quotation, publication,
  or house style overrides that default.
- Apply the skill lightly to substantive conversational prose. Leave short
  operational responses short.
- Repository instructions, authoritative protocols, project terminology, and
  explicit user requirements override this skill.

## Workflow

1. Identify the reader, purpose, intended tone, requested operation, and
   protected material.
2. Mark claims, qualifications, attributions, and terms whose alteration could
   change the meaning.
3. Scan for the patterns below. Revise only where the change reduces reader
   cost or corrects a genuine mismatch with the audience.
4. Compare the revision with the source. Restore any lost fact, qualification,
   position, useful structure, or deliberate stylistic choice.
5. Read once as the recipient. Fix any sentence that makes the reader decode
   the writer's process instead of receiving the point.

## Write for the reader

- Lead with the outcome, fact, request, or decision the reader needs.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs. Name the actor when the actor matters.
- Use a natural number of points. Vary sentence length when it helps the prose,
  not to perform informality.
- Express complexity through precise qualifications rather than fog or
  theatrical balance.
- Use first person when it truthfully identifies the author or owner of a view.
- Keep a neutral register when neutrality fits the evidence or document.

## Patterns to detect and fix

Treat these as diagnostic signs, not forbidden words or punctuation.

### Empty content

1. **Puffery and promotion.** Replace claims such as "pivotal", "breathtaking",
   "renowned", or "a testament to" with the fact that earns the description.
2. **Vague attribution.** Name the source and what it supports. If the source is
   unavailable, preserve an honest qualification or flag the unsupported claim;
   do not start research unless the task authorises it.
3. **Decorative participles.** Test "highlighting", "showcasing", "ensuring",
   and similar `-ing` clauses. Keep them only when they add a real relationship.
4. **Formulaic adversity.** Replace "despite challenges, X continues to thrive"
   with the actual problem, response, and result.
5. **Generic conclusions.** End with the decision, next action, remaining
   uncertainty, or measured result rather than "the future looks bright".

### Inflated language

6. **Prestige vocabulary.** Replace abstract uses of "landscape", "tapestry",
   "interplay", "pivotal", "underscore", and similar words when a plainer word
   carries the same meaning. Keep a precise technical or literal use.
7. **Ornate copulas.** Prefer "is" or "has" to "serves as", "stands as",
   "boasts", or "features" when nothing else changes.
8. **Abstract engineering metaphors.** Prefer the concrete mechanism to
   "substrate", "wedge", "vector", "locus", "primitive", "scaffolding",
   "ratchet", "flywheel", "north star", or "endgame" when those terms obscure
   what changes, where, or how.
9. **Fancy synonyms.** Prefer "use", "help", "many", and "if" to "utilise",
   "facilitate", "numerous", and "in the event that" unless the distinction is
   meaningful.

### Formula and rhythm

10. **Forced contrast.** Replace "not just X, but Y" when it merely dramatizes
    one point. Keep genuine correction or contrast.
11. **Forced threes.** Use the number of items the subject requires.
12. **Synonym cycling.** Repeat the established term when changing it would
    imply a distinction that does not exist.
13. **False ranges.** Use "from X to Y" only when X and Y occupy a meaningful
    scale or sequence.
14. **Dense sentences.** Split or shorten a sentence when the reader must
    backtrack. Do not reduce every sentence to one clause.
15. **Weak verb plus adverb.** Prefer the measured result or a more exact verb;
    retain the adverb when it carries real scope or degree.

### Presentation and conversational tells

16. **Punctuation as scaffolding.** Use colons, semicolons, parentheses, and em
    dashes when they clarify a real relationship. Remove repeated or mechanical
    use; preserve deliberate punctuation.
17. **Decorated structure.** Remove redundant bold labels, repeated inline
    headings, decorative emoji, and title case where sentence case reads more
    naturally. Keep formatting that helps navigation or follows house style.
18. **Chatbot ceremony.** Remove empty openings, praise, sycophancy, and stock
    closings. Respond to the substance.
19. **Filler and hedge stacks.** Prefer "to" to "in order to", "because" to
    "due to the fact that", and one accurate qualification to several nested
    possibilities.
20. **Feeling without mechanism.** Replace generic assurances or unease with the
    fact, mechanism, instruction, or number the reader can use. Keep genuine
    human reaction when the author owns it and it belongs in the document.

## Final audit

Ask:

- What does the reader need to understand or do?
- Which sentence makes that harder than necessary?
- Did any edit change the evidence, position, uncertainty, or protected text?
- Does the result sound appropriate for this reader rather than generically
  "human"?

Fix the remaining reader cost, then stop.
